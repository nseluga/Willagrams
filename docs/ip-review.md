# Distinctness review — Willagrams against Bananagrams

Written by the `style` lane alongside the visual identity. This is an
engineering record of why the shipped identity is defensible, not legal advice,
and it is worth a lawyer's hour before the first public build.

Scope: the name, the wordmark, the palette, the tile art, and the player-facing
vocabulary. It does not cover the rules — see [Mechanics](#mechanics-are-not-the-exposure).

---

## What Bananagrams actually owns

Three separate things, with very different strengths:

| Asset | Kind | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| BANANAGRAMS | Word mark | Strong. Coined, arbitrary as applied to a tile game, in continuous use since 2006. |
| The banana-shaped zippered pouch | Trade dress | Strong and famous. It is the product's whole shelf identity. |
| BANANAS, PEEL, DUMP, SPLIT, BUNCH, ROTTEN | Terms of play | Real but narrower. Distinctive **as a set** and as branded vocabulary; individually these are ordinary English words. |
| The bone-colored lettered tile | — | **Nothing.** Argued below. |

The fourth row is the one this document exists to establish, because it is the
only place where Willagrams and Bananagrams look alike at all.

## Mechanics are not the exposure

Draw-and-rearrange, one shared pool, a personal crossword grid, and a race to
place your last tile are **rules**. Rules and systems of play are outside
copyright (17 U.S.C. § 102(b); *Baker v. Selden*), and the courts that have
looked hardest at game clones — *Tetris Holding v. Xio*, *Spry Fox v. LOLApps* —
protected the *expression* of a game while explicitly leaving the rules free.

So the mechanics are not the risk, and the app should not be contorted to
disguise them. The risk is entirely in the expressive layer: what it is called,
what it looks like, and what words appear on screen. Those are what follow.

## The closest element: the beveled bone-colored tile face

Willagrams draws tiles as a `#EADCB6` face with a dark `#2C2718` letter, a 5pt
corner radius, and a three-point darker band along the bottom edge reading as a
bevel. Bananagrams' physical tiles are ivory plastic with dark letters, rounded
corners, and a molded chamfer. Rendered small, these are similar. That
similarity should be met directly rather than waved past.

### The two available arguments

**A. Category genericness.** A bone/ivory face with a dark letter and a
chamfered edge is the ordinary appearance of a letter tile across the whole
category, and has been since long before Bananagrams existed. It therefore
cannot function as any one company's source identifier.

**B. Point-by-point visual dissimilarity.** Willagrams' tiles differ in radius,
in exact hue, in the specific bevel treatment, in the type on the face, and in
lacking the point-value numeral some competitors carry.

### Which one carries the weight — and it is not the obvious one

**Argument A is the load-bearing one for the tile face. Argument B is close to
useless here, and leaning on it would be a mistake.**

Trade dress infringement is assessed on *overall commercial impression*, not on
a feature checklist — a defendant who wins nine of ten feature comparisons still
loses if the whole thing reads the same. A bare tile is a rectangle with a
letter on it. There is almost no surface area on which to be different, so a
point-by-point defense of a tile is a defense conducted on the plaintiff's best
ground: it silently concedes that the tile face is protectable and then argues
about degree. That is a losing posture even when every individual point is true.

Argument A refuses the premise instead. It says the element is not protectable
in the first place, on two independent grounds:

**1. Priority and ubiquity across the category.** The ivory-tile-with-dark-
letter convention is Scrabble's, from 1938 — nearly seventy years before
Bananagrams — and it runs through essentially every lettered-tile product since:
Upwords, Scrabble Junior, countless anagram and crossword-tile games, and the
generic blank letter tiles sold as craft supplies. A convention that predates a
claimant and is used industry-wide is generic for the category, and genericness
is a complete answer, not a mitigating factor.

**2. Functionality.** Trade dress protection does not reach features that are
functional (*TrafFix Devices v. Marketing Displays*). Every element of the tile
face is doing a job:

- **The pale face and dark letter** exist for maximum legibility contrast at
  small size and at a shallow reading angle across a table. This is the highest-
  contrast pairing that does not glare under room lighting.
- **The bevel** is, on a physical tile, a byproduct of manufacture — an injection
  mold needs draft angle, a wood or plastic edge is chamfered so it does not
  chip and so tiles do not catch when swept up. It is inherited by every molded
  tile whether or not anyone designed it.
- **The rounded corner** likewise: sharp corners chip and catch.
- **Ivory rather than white** because pure white is unpleasantly bright under
  warm light and shows wear.

A digital tile inherits this vocabulary because players read it as a tile. A
game whose tiles did *not* look like tiles would be worse, not more distinct,
and that is exactly the test functionality doctrine applies.

**Where argument B does belong.** Not on the tile — on the app. Overall
commercial impression is the right test at the level of the whole product, and
there Willagrams and Bananagrams are not close: no banana, no fruit of any kind,
no pouch, no yellow, a different name, a different wordmark, and a warm
gold-on-cream tabletop rather than Bananagrams' saturated yellow. The
point-by-point comparison is persuasive at that scale and unpersuasive at the
scale of one tile. Use it where it works.

**Practical hedge.** Willagrams' tile face is `#EADCB6` — a warm sand, visibly
more saturated and yellower than the near-white ivory of the physical product,
and it sits inside a warm gold-and-cream world rather than on a primary-yellow
one. This is a real difference in impression without compromising the
functional reasons for the convention. It is a hedge, not the argument.

## The rest of the identity, element by element

This is where argument B does its work.

### Name

| | Bananagrams | Willagrams |
|---|---|---|
| Sound | Five syllables, ba-NAN-a-grams | Four syllables, WILL-a-grams |
| Root | *banana* — the pouch, the mascot, the whole brand | *Willa* — a personal given name |
| Suffix | *-grams*, from ANAGRAMS | *-grams*, from ANAGRAMS |
| Meaning | Fruit | None; it reads as a person's name |

The shared element is `-grams`, and that is the weakest part of either mark:
it descends from *anagrams*, the generic description of what both games are, and
it is used across the category (Anagrams, Pathwords, Quiddler-adjacent naming,
and a long tail of word-game apps). Marks are compared as wholes, but a shared
generic or descriptive suffix is given little weight in that comparison. The
dominant, source-identifying portions — BANANA versus WILLA — share no letters,
no sound, and no meaning.

**This is the second-strongest exposure in the product**, after the tile face,
and it should be recorded as such rather than treated as settled. A knockout
search on WILLAGRAMS in Class 9 (downloadable game software) and Class 28
(games) is a prerequisite to filing, and the App Store listing should not use
"-grams" branding in a way that suggests a family of marks.

### Wordmark and palette

Bananagrams' identity is a banana: the pouch, the yellow, the cartoon fruit, the
peel-and-split language wrapped around it. Willagrams has no fruit and no
mascot. The wordmark is Instrument Sans Bold at tight tracking (−0.03em) —
typographic, not illustrative.

The palette is a warm tabletop rather than a primary yellow:

| Role | Willagrams | Bananagrams |
|---|---|---|
| Ground | Cream `#FAF4E2` → `#F3EAD2` | Saturated banana yellow |
| Accent | Muted gold `#C2841B` | Yellow, with green and brown accents |
| Text | Warm near-black `#2C2718` | Black on yellow |

Gold-on-cream and yellow are adjacent on the wheel, which is worth naming
honestly. They differ in the way that matters: Willagrams' gold is desaturated
and used sparingly as an accent on a neutral cream ground, while Bananagrams'
yellow is a saturated field color carrying the whole identity. The overall
impression is a wooden table under warm light, not a piece of fruit.

### Vocabulary

The vocabulary is the sharpest and cheapest distinction available, because it is
fully within our control and the difference is total. `Willagrams/Style/Terminology.swift`
is the single source of every player-facing game word:

| Concept | Willagrams | Bananagrams' term |
|---|---|---|
| The shared face-down supply | **Pool** | Bunch |
| Everyone takes one tile | **Draw** | Peel |
| Return one tile, take three | **Swap** | Dump |
| Deal the starting tiles | **Get ready** (a countdown, not a call) | Split |
| The win call | **Willagrams!** | Bananas! |
| A board that blocks the draw | **Invalid** | Rotten |

Most of these are ordinary, descriptive English words for what they do — Pool,
Draw, Swap, Invalid — which is deliberate: descriptive terms are weak marks for
*us* too, but they are also nearly impossible to be accused of appropriating.

**The honest weak point.** The win call is the one structural parallel left:
shouting the product's own name to win is Bananagrams' device, and "Willagrams!"
occupies the same slot that "Bananas!" does. The words themselves are not
similar, and a game's win call being its own title is a common enough convention
(Bingo, Uno, Yahtzee) that it is closer to *scènes à faire* than to
appropriation. It is defensible and it should stay — but it is the second thing
a reviewer will point at, and this document should not pretend otherwise.

## The fence, and the test that enforces it

These terms must never reach a player-facing string, in the app, in App Store
copy, in screenshots, or in support text:

```
bunch    split    peel    dump    bananas    rotten
```

This is enforced, not merely intended. `Tests/WillagramsRulesTests/TerminologyFenceTests.swift`
walks every `.swift` file under `Willagrams/`, strips comments, extracts string
literals, and fails the build if any literal contains one of those terms
case-insensitively. It carries its own planted-violation test, so the fence is
proven to detect rather than assumed to.

Two deliberate limits, both worth knowing:

- **It scans string literals only.** Comments and identifiers are exempt on
  purpose, so the fence can be documented in prose and so `text.split(separator:)`
  is not a false positive.
- **It scans `Willagrams/` only.** Store metadata, screenshots and marketing copy
  are outside its reach. The `release` lane owns those and needs its own check.

## Residual exposure, ranked

1. **The tile face.** Mitigated by genericness and functionality, which are
   strong arguments — but it remains the only visual element with real overlap.
2. **The `-grams` suffix.** Weak on the law, but it is what a trademark examiner
   or an opposition would seize on first. Clear a knockout search before filing.
3. **The win call.** Structurally parallel, verbally unrelated. Defensible.
4. **Store metadata.** Outside the automated fence entirely. Needs a manual pass
   before submission, and ideally a check of its own.
